Accessible parking isn't optional, and getting it wrong can mean fines, complaints, or worse — a visitor who can't safely reach your building. For property managers, the tricky part is that ADA parking rules are specific: a number of spaces, exact dimensions, particular signs, and an accessible route. Here's what to know, and how to tell whether your lot is in good shape.

<!--more-->

## How many accessible spaces do you need?

The ADA sets minimum accessible-space counts on a sliding scale based on the total number of spaces in the lot (or in each separate parking facility on the property). Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, a lot with 1–25 spaces needs at least 1 accessible space, 26–50 spaces need at least 2, 51–75 need at least 3, and so on, climbing steadily for larger lots. Very large lots are calculated as a percentage of the total.

Of the accessible spaces you provide, at least one in every six must be van-accessible. If you only need one accessible space, it should be a van space.

Local and state codes can be stricter than the federal minimum, and some require more spaces for specific uses such as medical offices. Check with your local code office or an accessibility consultant before finalizing a layout.

## What "compliant" actually means

Painting a blue symbol on a space isn't enough. A compliant accessible space is a combination of several pieces that all have to be right:

- **Width and access aisle.** A standard accessible space is at least 96 inches wide with an adjacent access aisle at least 60 inches wide.
- **Van-accessible spaces.** These need either a 132-inch-wide space with a 60-inch aisle, or a 96-inch space with a 96-inch aisle.
- **Level surface.** Spaces and access aisles must be nearly level; the ADA limits slope in any direction to about 2 percent. Years of settling, patching and sealcoating can change that.
- **Signage.** Each accessible space needs a sign showing the International Symbol of Accessibility, mounted so the bottom of the sign is at least 60 inches above the ground. Van spaces need an added "van accessible" designation.
- **Accessible route.** There must be a firm, stable, accessible path from the space to the building entrance — curb ramps where needed, no parked cars or planters blocking it, and no steps along the way.
- **Access aisle markings.** The aisle should be clearly marked (commonly with diagonal striping) so that no one parks in it.

## Where to place accessible spaces

Accessible spaces should be on the shortest accessible route to an accessible entrance. On a property with several entrances, spaces are typically spread out so that each entrance is served. A well-meaning layout that puts all of the accessible spaces at the far end of the lot, or on a slope, can fail even if every space is the correct size.

## A common mistake

Faded or incorrectly placed accessible markings are one of the most common compliance gaps we see. Often the original layout was correct, but repeated re-striping over the years slowly drifted from code as lines were repainted freehand, access aisles shrank, and symbols got touched up without being measured. Sealcoating that covers the old markings, followed by a rushed re-stripe, is another common way a compliant lot ends up non-compliant.

## A quick self-check for your property

Walk your lot with a tape measure and a short list:

1. Count your total spaces and your accessible spaces. Do the numbers match the ADA table (and any stricter local rule)?
2. Is at least one in six of the accessible spaces van-accessible?
3. Measure the widest and narrowest parts of each space and its access aisle.
4. Check that every space has a sign at the correct height, and that the signs aren't bent, faded or hidden by landscaping.
5. Trace the route from each space to the entrance. Is it clear, firm, level and ramped where it should be?
6. Look at the paint. Can the symbol and the aisle hatching be seen clearly from a car?

Anything that fails is worth fixing before it becomes a complaint or a demand letter.
